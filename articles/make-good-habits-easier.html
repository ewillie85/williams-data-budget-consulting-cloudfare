Make Good Habits Easier to Follow

What if building a better habit had less to do with willpower and more to do with making the right action easier to take?

We often talk about habits as if the answer is simply to try harder. Be more disciplined. Have more willpower. Stick to the plan.

And when we don't follow through, it's easy to decide that the problem is us.

But before making that judgment, consider another possibility: maybe the way we've set things up is making the habit harder than it needs to be.

Look at what happens before the habit

Our actions don't happen in isolation. They're influenced by what's around us, what we're doing at the time, and how much effort a particular choice requires.

If you want to spend less time scrolling on your phone, for example, think about when and where you reach for it. Is it beside you every time you sit down? Is it the first thing you see when you wake up? Is opening an app easier than deciding what else to do with a spare five minutes?

Instead of relying entirely on willpower in that moment, you could move the phone out of reach, turn off unnecessary notifications, or decide on another activity before the urge appears.

The point isn't that one technique works for everyone. It's that understanding the circumstances around a habit gives you more options than simply telling yourself to stop.

Add friction to the habits you want to reduce

Sometimes a small barrier is enough to interrupt an automatic choice.

You might remove shopping apps from your home screen, unsubscribe from promotional emails that encourage impulse purchases, or avoid saving payment details on websites where you tend to buy things without much thought.

These changes don't remove your ability to make a choice. They create a pause between the urge and the action—a little room to decide whether the purchase or behavior actually fits your priorities.

That pause can be useful. It gives you a chance to ask a better question than "Can I do this?" You can ask, "Is this what I want to do right now?"

Make the habits you want easier to start

The same idea works in the other direction. If you want to build a habit, reduce the effort required to begin.

Want to read more? Leave a book somewhere you'll see it. Want to prepare meals at home more often? Plan a few simple meals and make sure the ingredients are available. Want to review your spending regularly? Choose a consistent time and keep the information you need easy to access.

Notice how small these adjustments can be. You don't need to redesign your life in one weekend. You're simply making the desired action a little more convenient.

When a new habit requires too many decisions or too much preparation, it's easy to put it off. Simplifying the first step can make it easier to get started.

Start with a reason that matters to you

There's another question worth asking before trying to change a habit: why do you want to change it in the first place?

It's easy to adopt goals because they're popular, because someone else recommends them, or because we think we should be doing what everyone around us seems to be doing.

But a habit is more likely to feel worthwhile when it's connected to something you genuinely value. Maybe you're trying to spend more intentionally so you have greater freedom to travel. Maybe you want a simpler routine that leaves more time for family, creative work, or rest.

Understanding the reason behind the change helps you decide which habits deserve your attention—and which expectations you can let go.

Make one change, then learn from it

It's tempting to tackle everything at once. A new routine, a new schedule, a new budget, and a long list of promises to yourself can feel motivating at the beginning.

But you don't need to change everything to make progress.

Choose one habit you'd like to make easier or one you'd like to reduce. Pay attention to what tends to trigger it. Make a small adjustment to your surroundings or routine, and see what happens.

If it doesn't work, that doesn't mean you've failed. It means you've learned something about the approach you tried. Adjust it and try again, or consider whether the habit is as important to you as you first thought.

Give yourself some patience along the way. The goal isn't to build a life where every action is perfectly optimized. It's to make useful changes without turning self-improvement into another source of pressure.

Less struggle, more intention

Willpower has its place, but it doesn't have to carry the entire burden of change. The way we arrange our surroundings, the decisions we make in advance, and the reasons behind our goals all influence how easy it is to follow through.

Rather than asking yourself to try harder every day, look for ways to make the actions you value easier to take—and the habits you want to reduce a little less automatic.

You might discover that a small adjustment is more useful than another ambitious plan.

Keep cutting the noise and make room for what's essential.
