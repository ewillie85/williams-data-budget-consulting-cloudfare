More Data Doesn't Always Mean Better Decisions

Before building another dashboard or adding another report, it helps to ask a simpler question: what decision are we actually trying to make?

We live in a world where collecting information has become easier than ever. Businesses can track sales, customer activity, operational performance, marketing campaigns, and just about anything else they can think to measure.

That sounds like a good thing. And often, it is. But there's a question worth asking: does having more information automatically help us make better decisions?

Not necessarily.

Start with the question, not the dashboard

Imagine a business reviewing its performance every month. The team has a dashboard filled with charts, percentages, comparisons, and trend lines. It looks polished. The numbers are updated. Everyone has plenty to look at.

But after the meeting, what changes?

If the team can't identify what needs attention, why it matters, or what action to take next, the dashboard may be reporting activity without helping the business move forward.

This is why I believe a useful data project should begin with a clear business question. Are we trying to understand why costs are rising? Identify which customers are returning? Find delays in a process? Decide where to focus limited resources?

The question shapes the analysis. Without it, it's easy to spend time collecting and presenting information simply because it's available.

More information can create more noise

Every additional metric asks for some of our attention. We have to understand what it measures, whether it is reliable, how it relates to other numbers, and whether it deserves to influence a decision.

When everything is presented as equally important, the most important things can become harder to see.

The answer isn't to avoid data. It's to be more intentional about which data we use and why. A small set of relevant measures can be more useful than a crowded report full of numbers that don't lead anywhere.

Connect the insight to an action

A helpful analysis should do more than describe what happened. It should help someone decide what to do with that information.

That doesn't mean every chart needs to produce an immediate answer. Sometimes the right next step is to investigate further, check an assumption, or gather better information. But the path from the business question to the analysis and then to the next step should be clear.

When building a report or dashboard, I find it useful to ask:

  • Who will use this information?
  • What question are they trying to answer?
  • What would they do differently based on what they learn?
  • Which measures are essential, and which are just adding noise?

Make room for what matters

Good business intelligence isn't about measuring everything. It's about making the important things easier to understand.

That takes some discipline. It means being willing to question whether a report is useful, whether a metric supports a real decision, and whether a more complicated solution is actually a better one.

Before adding another chart, another metric, or another dashboard, take a breath and start with the question.

Cut the noise and make room for what's essential.
